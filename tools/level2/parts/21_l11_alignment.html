Lesson 11Sequence alignment

🧬 Why this matters in bioinformatics

Comparing sequences is at the heart of bioinformatics. Is this patient's gene different from the reference? Which gene does this read come from? How closely related are the human and mouse versions of a protein? Answering any of these means lining two sequences up so that matching parts sit on top of each other. That's an alignment. Read mappers, BLAST searches and evolutionary trees are all built on it.

The idea: line them up

Imagine writing two sequences on strips of paper and sliding them until as many letters as possible line up. Three things can happen in each column:

ATGGTGCATCTG
|||||-||.|||       | = match    . = mismatch    - = gap
ATGGT-CACCTG
  • A match: the same base in both sequences.
  • A mismatch: different bases, for example a point mutation or a sequencing error.
  • A gap (-): a base in one sequence has no partner in the other, because a base was inserted in one or deleted from the other. Together these are called indels.

Scoring: how good is an alignment?

To compare different ways of lining sequences up, we give each column points and add them up. A simple scoring scheme:

ColumnScoreWhy
match+1evidence the sequences are related
mismatch−1mutations happen, but they cost something
gap−2indels are rarer than point mutations, so they cost more

The alignment above has 10 matches, 1 mismatch and 1 gap: 10 − 1 − 2 = 7. Different scoring schemes can give different "best" alignments, so always say which one you used.

You don't need to know how these methods work inside (they're clever, and they're guaranteed to find the best-scoring alignment). Biopython does it for you.

Aligning with Biopython: spot the sickle cell mutation

🤓 Did you know?

Geneticists have an official way to write mutations, called HGVS notation. The sickle cell mutation is written NM_000518.5:c.20A>T: in the transcript NM_000518.5, coding base (c.) number 20 changes from A to T. At the protein level it's p.Glu7Val, though you'll often see the older name Glu6Val, which doesn't count the first methionine (remember Level 1?).

🧪 Project: human versus mouse beta-globin

Humans and mice shared a common ancestor about 75–90 million years ago. How much has beta-globin changed since then? Here are the first 90 bases of the coding sequence from each species:

About 86% identical, with no gaps. Now look at where the 13 mismatches fall: 5 of them are in the third base of a codon (bases 9, 33, 36, 51 and 63). Across the whole coding sequence the pattern is even clearer: 40 of the 77 differences are in third positions, although only a third of all bases are third bases, and 29 of the 77 don't change the amino acid at all. Thanks to the redundancy of the genetic code, these "silent" changes leave the protein untouched, so evolution lets them pile up. Changes that alter the protein are much more often weeded out.

To compare the proteins themselves, align the amino acid sequences. They're 80% identical over all 147 amino acids. 🧹 Good habit: report your settings

An alignment score means nothing without the scoring scheme. When you report one, write down the mode (global or local), the match, mismatch and gap scores (or the substitution matrix), and the Biopython version. Better still, keep them as named settings at the top of your script.

Quick quiz

1. You want to find where a 100-base read fits inside a 10,000-base gene. Which mode?

  • Global alignment would try to match the read along the whole gene, adding huge gaps.
  • Right: local alignment finds the best-matching region and ignores the rest.
  • You can. Local alignment is made for exactly this.

2. With match +1, mismatch −1, gap −2, what is the score of ACG-T over ACGAT?

  • The gap column costs −2, it doesn't score +1.
  • Yes: 4 matches (+4) and 1 gap (−2) = 2.
  • A gap costs −2 here, not −1.

3. Many differences between human and mouse beta-globin DNA are in the third base of codons. Why does evolution tolerate those so easily?

  • Exactly. Such "silent" changes leave the protein the same, so they're rarely harmful.
  • It does read it: TGG is tryptophan but TGA is a stop! The third base just often doesn't matter, because of redundancy.
  • Mice and humans use the same standard genetic code for nuclear genes.
✅ Key takeaways
  • An alignment lines up sequences into columns of matches, mismatches and gaps.
  • A scoring scheme (e.g. +1 / −1 / −2, or BLOSUM62 for proteins) decides which alignment is best. Always report it.
  • Global aligns end to end (similar sequences); local finds the best-matching region (a read in a gene).
  • PairwiseAligner: set .mode and the scores, then aligner.align(a, b)[0]; use .score, .counts(), and alignment[0] / alignment[1] for the rows.
