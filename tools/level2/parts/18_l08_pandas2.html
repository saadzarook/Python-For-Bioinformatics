Lesson 8Tables with pandas, part 2

🧬 Why this matters in bioinformatics

Single numbers rarely tell the story. Biologists ask questions about groups: do p53 target genes go up on average? Which pathway changes most? The answers usually need two tables joined together, such as your measurements plus a table that says what each gene does. And real tables have holes in them. This lesson covers all three: summaries, joining tables and missing values.

Summary statistics

.describe() gives a quick summary of every number column: how many values there are, the mean, the spread and the quartiles. It's a great first look at any new table.

A few things jump out. count is 14 for treated_tpm but 15 for control: one value is missing. And the mean (about 400) is far above the median (42 and 87), because two very highly expressed genes, GAPDH and ACTB, pull the mean up. Gene expression is almost always like this: a few genes are hugely expressed and most are modest. The median is the more typical value.

Merging two tables

Our expression table says how much each gene changed. The annotation table, annotation.tsv (also made-up teaching data, built from real gene facts), says what each gene does: its chromosome and its pathway. To ask "which pathways changed?", we need both together. merge() joins two tables using a column they share, here gene:

For every gene in the expression table, pandas found the matching row in the annotation table and added its columns. Look at TP53I3: it isn't in the annotation table, so its new columns are NaN. That's what how="left" means: keep every row of the left table (the one before .merge), even without a match.

💡 What's the ... in my table?

When a table is too wide to print, pandas hides the middle columns and shows ... instead, with a note such as [15 rows x 7 columns]. Nothing is lost: it's only hidden from the printout. Select the columns you want to look at, as in merged[["gene", "pathway"]].

⚠️ Check your row counts after a merge

A merge can quietly lose rows (with "inner") or even multiply them (if a gene appears twice in one table). Always compare len() before and after. Gene names are also a classic trap: TP53 and tp53, or an old name versus a new one, won't match.

Group and summarise: groupby

groupby splits a table into groups by a column, calculates something for each group, and puts the results back together. "For each pathway, what's the average log2 fold change?" is one line:

Read the first line as: group merged by pathway, take the log2_fc column, and calculate the mean for each group. .agg() calculates several things at once. The story is clear: p53 pathway genes went up (about 1.9, almost 4 times more), cell-cycle genes went down, and housekeeping genes stayed put.

Two details: count for the p53 pathway is 5, not 6, because FAS has no fold change, and count only counts real values. And TP53I3, which has no pathway, isn't in any group. Missing values are left out of groups unless you ask otherwise.

🤓 Did you know?

TP53 itself barely changed (log2 fold change 0.1), even though it's the gene in charge of this whole response! That's realistic: after DNA damage, cells don't make much more p53 mRNA. Instead, the p53 protein stops being destroyed. Normally MDM2 tags p53 for destruction; DNA damage blocks that. Expression data shows you mRNA levels, which isn't always the whole story.

Saving your results

.to_csv() writes a DataFrame to a file. Add index=False so the row numbers aren't saved as an extra column, and remember Lesson 6: write to a new file, never over the raw data.

Quick quiz

1. In .describe(), the mean TPM is about 400 but the median is 42. What does that tell you?

  • Right. Expression data is "skewed", so the median is the more typical value.
  • Not necessarily. This is normal for gene expression: a handful of genes are expressed enormously.
  • They're only equal for symmetrical data.

2. Table A has 15 genes, table B has 14 of them. How many rows does A.merge(B, on="gene", how="inner") have?

  • That would be how="left". "inner" keeps only genes found in both tables.
  • Yes: only the 14 shared genes. The missing one disappears without any warning, which is why you should check row counts.
  • merge joins rows side by side; it doesn't stack the two tables.

3. What does df.groupby("pathway")["log2_fc"].mean() give?

  • That would be df["log2_fc"].mean(). groupby gives one mean per group.
  • Correct: split by pathway, average each group, combine the results.
  • That's sort_values("pathway"). groupby summarises groups.
✅ Key takeaways
  • .describe(), .mean() and .median() summarise columns; the median is more typical for skewed data such as expression.
  • Find missing values with .isna().sum(); handle them on purpose with .dropna() or .fillna(), and say what you did.
  • a.merge(b, on="gene", how="left") joins tables; check the row count afterwards.
  • df.groupby("col")["value"].mean() summarises each group; .agg([...]) does several summaries at once.
  • Save with .to_csv("new_file.csv", index=False).
