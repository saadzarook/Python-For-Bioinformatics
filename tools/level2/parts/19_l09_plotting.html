Lesson 9Plotting with matplotlib

🧬 Why this matters in bioinformatics

A table of 40 numbers is hard to read; a picture of them takes one second. Plots are how you check your data (is the quality falling? is there an odd sample?) and how you show results to others, in lab meetings, posters and papers. A good plot is honest and easy to read; a careless one can mislead, even by accident.

💡 Loading matplotlib

matplotlib is Python's most widely used plotting library. Your first plot

Everyone imports the plotting part of matplotlib as plt. A plot is built in steps: draw the data, then add labels, then show it.

Every plot on this page also gets a short text description for screen readers, made from its title and axis labels. One more reason to always label your plots!

Line plots show how something changes along an ordered axis, such as position or time. Let's average the quality at each position across all 40 reads. pandas can do this in one step: put the scores in a DataFrame with one row per read and one column per position, and .mean() averages each column.

There's the classic pattern from Lesson 5: quality starts above Q30 and falls towards the 3′ end. A few new things here: plt.axhline() draws a horizontal reference line; label= plus plt.legend() adds a key; and color="C1" means "the second colour of the current style" ("C0" is the first), so colours stay consistent and readable.

Scatter plots: two measurements at once

A scatter plot puts one dot per item, with one measurement across and another up. Here, every gene's control TPM against its treated TPM. Expression values span from 0.1 to 3000, so we use logarithmic axes, where each step is ten times bigger than the last. Genes on the dashed diagonal didn't change.

Dots above the diagonal went up (CDKN1A), dots below went down (MYC), and GAPDH sits right on it. plt.annotate() writes a label next to a point; label only a few interesting points, not every one.

What makes a plot honest and readable?

🧹 Good habit: a checklist for every plot
  • Label both axes, with units: "GC content (%)", not "gc".
  • Give it a title that says what it shows, and how many items (n = 40 reads).
  • Bar charts start at zero. A bar's length is its value; cutting off the bottom exaggerates differences.
  • Say when a scale is logarithmic, in the axis label.
  • Use colour for meaning (up vs down), not decoration, and pick colours that colour-blind readers can tell apart. Never rely on colour alone: add a legend or labels.
  • Skip 3D effects and dual y-axes. They make values harder to read and easier to misread.
  • Keep the code that made the plot, so the figure can be remade exactly. Never edit a figure by hand.

Here's what a truncated axis does. Both panels show the same two numbers. The left one makes ACTB look about four times higher than GAPDH; it's really 1.4 times. (plt.subplot(1, 2, 1) means "a grid of 1 row and 2 columns: draw in panel 1".)

🤓 Did you know?

About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some form of colour blindness, most often difficulty telling red from green. The classic red-green "heatmap" used in many older gene expression papers is unreadable for them. That's why modern plots use colour-blind-safe palettes, like the blue and orange on this page.

Quick quiz

1. You want to see how the GC content of 10,000 reads is distributed. Which plot?

  • Right: a histogram shows how many values fall in each range.
  • Line plots are for values along an ordered axis, like position or time. The reads have no natural order.
  • 10,000 bars would be unreadable. A histogram groups the values into bins.

2. Why should the y-axis of a bar chart start at zero?

  • It can (with plt.ylim), and that's exactly the problem.
  • Exactly. A truncated axis can make a 1.4× difference look like 4×.
  • It's about honesty, not size.

3. Expression values range from 0.1 to 3000 TPM. What helps a scatter plot show them all clearly?

  • Yes: on a log scale, 0.1, 1, 10, 100 and 1000 are evenly spaced. Always say the scale is logarithmic.
  • Removing data to make a plot look nicer is not honest. Change the scale instead.
  • That doesn't solve the problem of values that differ by thousands of times.
✅ Key takeaways
  • import matplotlib.pyplot as plt; draw with plt.hist(), plt.plot(), plt.bar() or plt.scatter(), then plt.show().
  • Always add plt.xlabel(), plt.ylabel() (with units) and plt.title(); use label= and plt.legend() for several series.
  • Pick the plot for the job: histogram for distributions, line for ordered positions, bar for categories, scatter for two measurements.
  • Honest plots: bar charts start at 0, log scales are labelled, colours carry meaning and are colour-blind safe.
  • Keep the code that makes every figure; save with plt.savefig() on your own computer.
